DAV Chapter 4 — Topic 1

What is Force?

A force is a push or a pull. It is an external agent that can change the state, shape, size, speed, or direction of an object.

Definition (write this in exams): Force is defined as an external agent which tries to bring a change in the state of rest or motion, or the shape/size of an object.

Push

Applying force away from yourself. Pushing a door open, pushing a box across the floor, pushing a swing.

Pull

Applying force towards yourself. Drawing water from a well, pulling a drawer open, stretching a rubber band.

Magnitude & Direction

Force has both magnitude (how strong) and direction (which way). Changing either one changes the effect of the force.

Important: For a force to come into play, two bodies must interact with each other — either directly (contact) or through a field (non-contact).
Force = mass × acceleration
Unit: Newton (N)
1 Newton = force needed to give 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²
DAV Chapter 4 — Topic 2

Effects of Force

A force can produce four distinct effects on an object. You must know all four with real-life examples for the exam.

1

Make a stationary object move

A ball at rest starts moving when you kick it. A stalled car starts moving when pushed. Force brings rest to motion.

2

Stop a moving object

A goalkeeper catches a ball — the ball stops. Applying brakes stops a bicycle. Force brings motion to rest.

3

Change the speed of a moving object

Force in the direction of motion increases speed. Force opposite to motion decreases speed.

4

Change the direction of motion

A cricket bat hits a moving ball at an angle — the ball changes direction. Force acting at an angle to motion changes direction.

5

Change the shape or size of an object

Squeezing clay changes its shape. Stretching a rubber band changes its size. Crushing a tin can changes its shape.

DAV exam note: The chapter groups effects into two: (1) change in state of motion, and (2) change in shape/size. Make sure you know examples for both groups.

DAV Chapter 4 — Topic 3

Balanced & Unbalanced Forces

When multiple forces act on an object, their combined effect determines what happens. The key is the net (resultant) force.

Balanced Forces

  • Net force on the object = zero
  • Object does not accelerate
  • If at rest, stays at rest
  • If moving, continues at same speed
  • May change shape (but not motion)
  • Example: tug of war where neither side moves
  • Example: book lying on a table

Unbalanced Forces

  • Net force on the object is not zero
  • Object accelerates in direction of net force
  • If at rest, starts to move
  • If moving, changes speed or direction
  • Example: one team wins tug of war
  • Example: a kicked football flying through air
  • Example: car accelerating on a road
Key formula: Net Force = Force 1 + Force 2 (taking direction into account). Forces in opposite directions are subtracted. If net force = 0, forces are balanced.
Book on table
Weight down = Normal force up. Balanced. Book stays still.
Tug of war
Equal pull both sides = balanced. Unequal = one side wins (unbalanced).
Floating boat
Weight down = buoyancy up. Balanced. Boat stays afloat.
Rocket launching
Thrust up > weight down. Unbalanced. Rocket accelerates upward.

DAV Chapter 4 — Topic 5

Contact Forces

Contact forces require the two objects to physically touch each other. The most important contact forces in this chapter are muscular force and friction.

Muscular Force

Force exerted by muscles in our body. All physical activities — lifting, throwing, pushing, cycling, writing — use muscular force.

Friction

A force that opposes relative motion between two surfaces in contact. Acts opposite to the direction of motion. It is both useful and harmful.

Normal Force

The perpendicular push from a surface on an object resting on it. A book on a table is pushed up by the normal force of the table.

Friction facts (DAV important): Friction depends on the nature of surfaces and the force pressing them together. Smooth surfaces have less friction. Rough surfaces have more friction.
Useful friction: Walking (feet grip floor), writing (pen grips paper), brakes stopping vehicles, holding objects.
Harmful friction: Wear and tear of machine parts, tyres wearing out, energy loss as heat.
Reducing friction: Lubricants (oil, grease), ball bearings, smooth polished surfaces, streamlining.
Increasing friction: Rough surfaces on tyres, treads on shoes, grooves on handles, sand on icy roads.
DAV Chapter 4 — Topic 6

Non-Contact Forces

Non-contact forces act between objects without any physical touch. They act through fields — gravitational, magnetic, and electric fields.

Gravitational Force

Every object with mass attracts every other object. Earth pulls everything downward. The larger the mass, the greater the gravity.

Magnetic Force

Force between magnets or between a magnet and magnetic material (iron, nickel, cobalt). Like poles repel, unlike poles attract.

Electrostatic Force

Force between electrically charged objects. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract. Created by rubbing — comb attracting paper bits.

DAV Chapter 4 — Topic 7

What is Pressure?

Pressure describes how a force is distributed over an area. The same force over a smaller area creates much more pressure.

Thrust vs Pressure: Thrust is the force applied perpendicular to a surface. Pressure is the thrust per unit area of that surface.
Pressure = Force / Area
P = Pressure (Pa or N/m²)  |  F = Force / Thrust (N)  |  A = Area (m²)
1 Pascal = 1 N/m²
Golden rule: Same force, smaller area = MORE pressure. Same force, larger area = LESS pressure. This is why a knife cuts easily and why snowshoes stop you from sinking.

DAV Chapter 4 — Topic 8

Liquid Pressure

Liquids exert pressure on all surfaces they are in contact with. The pressure increases with depth and with the density of the liquid.

Deeper = more pressure

The weight of liquid above pushes down on everything below. More depth = more liquid above = greater pressure.

Acts in all directions

Unlike solids, liquids exert pressure equally upward, downward, and sideways. A hole at any point on a submerged container will leak.

Denser liquid = more pressure

Salt water exerts more pressure than fresh water at the same depth. Mercury exerts far more pressure than water.

P = ρ × g × h
P = liquid pressure (Pa)  |  ρ = density of liquid (kg/m³)
g = 10 m/s²  |  h = depth below surface (m)

Submarines — hull is engineered to resist the crushing water pressure when submerged.
DAV Chapter 4 — Topic 9

Atmospheric Pressure

The air around us has weight. This weight pushes on all surfaces — that is atmospheric pressure. It acts in all directions, just like liquid pressure.

Definition: The pressure exerted by the atmosphere (air) on all objects on Earth's surface is called atmospheric pressure. At sea level: approximately 101,325 Pa = 1 atm.

Decreases with altitude

Higher up, there is less air above you. So atmospheric pressure is lower at mountain tops than at sea level. Ears pop in aeroplanes.

Acts in all directions

Air pushes from all sides equally. We don't feel it because the pressure inside our body balances it out perfectly.

Measured by barometer

A barometer measures atmospheric pressure. Mercury barometer: normal atmospheric pressure supports a column of mercury 76 cm tall.

Drinking through a straw: You reduce pressure inside the straw by sucking. Atmospheric pressure outside pushes the liquid up.
Suction cups: Pressing a suction cup removes air, creating low pressure. Atmospheric pressure outside holds the cup firmly to the surface.
Tyres and footballs: Pumped up with air at pressure greater than atmospheric. The air inside pushes outward, keeping the tyre firm.
Weather changes: Falling atmospheric pressure usually indicates rain or storms coming. Rising pressure means fair weather ahead.
DAV exam favourite: Why doesn't the atmosphere crush us? Because the pressure inside our bodies equals the atmospheric pressure outside — they balance each other exactly.

Density

Density tells you how much mass is packed into a given volume. It directly determines whether objects float or sink in a liquid.

ρ = m / V
ρ (rho) = density (kg/m³)  |  m = mass (kg)  |  V = volume (m³)
Rearranged:   m = ρ × V    V = m / ρ
Float or sink rule: If an object's density is less than water (1000 kg/m³) it floats. If greater, it sinks. Wood (700 kg/m³) floats. Iron (7874 kg/m³) sinks.

Class 9 — Topic 1

Inertia — The Tendency to Resist Change

Before Newton's Laws, you need to understand one idea: objects are lazy. They do not like to change what they are doing. This laziness has a name — inertia.

Simple definition: Inertia is the natural tendency of an object to resist any change in its state of rest or uniform motion. It is not a force — it is a property of matter.

Inertia of rest

A stationary object resists being set in motion. You have to push a heavy table to get it moving — it resists you. The heavier the table, the more it resists.

Inertia of motion

A moving object resists being stopped or slowed. A cricket ball bowled at 140 km/h carries enormous inertia — it is hard to stop it quickly.

Inertia of direction

A moving object resists changing direction. Passengers in a car lean sideways when the car takes a sharp turn — their bodies want to keep going straight.

Key fact: Inertia depends entirely on mass. Greater mass = greater inertia. A truck is harder to start moving and harder to stop than a bicycle — because the truck has far more mass.
Dust off a carpet
Beat the carpet — carpet moves, dust stays behind (inertia of rest).
Bus brakes suddenly
You lurch forward — your body keeps moving while the bus stops (inertia of motion).
Coin on cardboard
Flick the cardboard — coin stays in place due to inertia of rest, falls into glass.
Car takes sharp turn
You lean outward — body resists direction change (inertia of direction).
Athlete runs past finish line
Cannot stop instantly — inertia of motion keeps them running forward.
Stone in a sling
Released — stone flies in a straight line due to inertia of direction.
Class 9 — Topic 2

Newton's First Law of Motion

Now that you understand inertia, Newton's First Law is simply a formal statement of it. This law is sometimes called the Law of Inertia.

Newton's First Law (write this in exams): An object remains in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless it is acted upon by an external unbalanced force.

What the law says

  • A stationary object stays still forever unless a force acts on it
  • A moving object keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction forever — unless a force acts on it
  • Only an unbalanced (net) force can cause a change
  • This is why space probes keep travelling without any engine once launched

Common misconception

  • On Earth, moving objects do slow down and stop
  • But that is because friction and air resistance are acting on them
  • Remove all forces — the object would move forever
  • Newton's law describes what happens with zero net force, not just zero applied force
Galileo's contribution: Newton built on Galileo's experiments showing that objects on frictionless surfaces keep moving indefinitely. Newton formalised this observation into a law.
Uniform motion means constant speed in a straight line — velocity does not change in magnitude or direction.
Natural state of matter is not rest — it is whatever it is already doing. Rest is just one possible state.
Connect to Class 8: Remember balanced forces from Class 8? When forces are balanced (net force = zero), Newton's First Law says nothing changes. That is exactly what you already knew — it just has a name now.
Class 9 — Topic 3

Momentum

Before Newton's Second Law, you need one more concept — momentum. It combines both mass and velocity into a single quantity that tells you how hard it is to stop something.

Simple idea: A heavy truck moving slowly and a tennis ball moving very fast can both be hard to stop — just in different ways. Momentum captures both these situations in one number.
p = m × v
p = momentum (kg·m/s)  |  m = mass (kg)  |  v = velocity (m/s)
Momentum is a vector — it has direction (same as velocity)

Large mass, low speed

A truck of 5000 kg moving at 2 m/s has momentum = 10,000 kg·m/s. Hard to stop because of its mass.

Small mass, high speed

A bullet of 0.01 kg moving at 500 m/s has momentum = 5 kg·m/s. Dangerous because of its speed — concentrated in tiny area.

Zero momentum

An object at rest has zero momentum (v = 0). Momentum only exists when something is moving.

Class 9 — Topic 4

Newton's Second Law of Motion

The First Law told you that unbalanced force causes change. The Second Law tells you exactly how much change — it gives you the formula.

Newton's Second Law: The rate of change of momentum of an object is directly proportional to the applied unbalanced force, and takes place in the direction of the force.
F = m × a
F = Force (N)  |  m = mass (kg)  |  a = acceleration (m/s²)
1 Newton = force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²
F = (mv − mu) / t
The full form — Force = change in momentum / time
mv = final momentum  |  mu = initial momentum

Direct proportion to force

  • Double the force → double the acceleration
  • Triple the force → triple the acceleration
  • Same mass — more force always means more acceleration
  • Push a trolley gently vs push it hard

Inverse proportion to mass

  • Double the mass → half the acceleration
  • Same force on a car vs a bicycle — bicycle accelerates far more
  • Heavy objects are harder to accelerate
  • This is exactly the inertia idea from Topic 1

Class 9 — Topic 5

Newton's Third Law of Motion

The First Law was about rest and motion. The Second Law gave you the formula. Now the Third Law explains something that will surprise you — forces never come alone.

Newton's Third Law: To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. The two forces always act on different objects — never on the same object.
The most important point: Action and reaction forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction — but they act on different objects. This is why they do NOT cancel each other out.

Rocket propulsion

Rocket pushes gases downward (action). Gases push rocket upward (reaction). The rocket moves up because the reaction acts on the rocket.

Walking

Your foot pushes the ground backward (action). Ground pushes your foot forward (reaction). You move forward because of the ground's reaction.

Gun recoil

Gun pushes bullet forward (action). Bullet pushes gun backward (reaction). The gun kicks backward — that is recoil.

Swimming

You push water backward with your hands (action). Water pushes you forward (reaction). That is how you move through water.

Classic exam question — "If action = reaction, why does anything move?" Because action and reaction act on different objects. When you push a wall, the wall pushes you back — you move backward, not the wall (because the wall is fixed to the ground which has enormous mass and inertia).
Class 9 — Topic 6

Conservation of Momentum

This is the grand conclusion that follows from Newton's Third Law. It is one of the most powerful laws in all of physics — and it is simpler than it sounds.

Law of Conservation of Momentum: In the absence of an external unbalanced force, the total momentum of a system of objects remains constant (is conserved).

In plain words: whatever momentum two objects have before a collision, they have the same total momentum after the collision. One object may give momentum to the other, but none is created or destroyed.

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂
m₁, m₂ = masses of the two objects
u₁, u₂ = initial velocities  |  v₁, v₂ = final velocities
Total momentum before = Total momentum after

3

Solve for the unknown velocity

You will be given one unknown — substitute the known values and solve.

Equal mass collision

Unequal masses
Heavy ball hits lighter ball — lighter ball moves fast, heavy ball slows but continues.
Rocket in space
Before: rocket + gas, both at rest. After: gas goes one way, rocket goes the other. Total momentum = 0 still.
Gun firing
Before: gun + bullet at rest (p=0). After: bullet forward + gun backward. Total still = 0.
Connect everything: Conservation of momentum follows directly from Newton's Third Law. Action force changes momentum of object A. Reaction force (equal, opposite) changes momentum of object B by exactly the same amount in the opposite direction. Total change = zero. Total momentum = constant.
